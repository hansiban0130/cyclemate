1Concept note

1.1 The health problem

Infertility affects roughly one in six adults worldwide (WHO, 2023). In vitro fertilisation (IVF) is an effective treatment, but it only works for patients who complete the cycle — and a substantial share do not.

The gap CycleMate addresses: up to one in five IVF patients discontinue treatment before completing it, and the leading reason is not medical failure or poor prognosis but the burden of the treatment itself. Most patients who drop out do not seek care elsewhere afterwards (Domar et al., 2018) — so treatable infertility is left untreated.

Anxiety is not constant: in the E-Freeze randomised trial (UK) both women and men were more anxious at embryo transfer than at the start of treatment. The hardest days come late in the cycle, when patients have the fewest clinic contacts.

The disease is infertility. CycleMate does not treat it; it removes the reasons why treatable patients stop being treated.

1.2 What "burden" is, concretely

From the literature and from day-to-day experience at a fertility clinic in Tashkent, the burden has four recurring components:

  1. Uncertainty — patients do not know what happens next, how long each phase lasts, or whether a symptom is normal.
  2. Daily load — clinic visits for stimulation injections, home medication (oral tablets, vaginal progesterone), and a trigger injection that must be given at an exact time, usually at night.
  3. Feeling alone between visits — distress builds silently; patients rarely ask for help, they simply stop coming.
  4. Nowhere to ask a small question — contact runs through phone calls and messenger DMs, so small questions either go unasked or land on the wrong person.

Two further pressures are specific to the Uzbekistan context: distance (many patients live hundreds of kilometres from Tashkent and cannot attend daily) and cost uncertainty, which in some settings is the single largest reason for discontinuation (Kulkarni et al., 2014: 62.5%).

2Prototype and design choices

2.1 Form: a web app, not a native app

CycleMate is built as a mobile web application. This is an equity decision before it is a technical one: it runs on any low-end Android phone through the browser, needs no app-store account, no storage space and no update, and can fall back to SMS for reminders when the patient is offline. For a clickable prototype the same choice means the demo runs from a single HTML file on any laptop or phone.

2.3 Oy — the companion, and why it is a moon

The single most important UX decision in CycleMate is the character Oy ("moon" in Uzbek), and it was chosen for three reasons.

  1. A lunar month and an IVF cycle are both about 28 days. Oy waxes through ovarian stimulation, is full at embryo transfer, and wanes toward the pregnancy test. One glance at the moon tells a patient where she is in the cycle — without a calendar, without reading, in any language.
  2. Calm by design. Medical apps commonly rely on alerts, red badges and countdowns, all of which raise anxiety in a population whose anxiety is already clinically elevated. Oy replaces those cues with a soft, familiar presence that reacts to how the patient feels: it says "Heavy day. That's allowed." on a low check-in, and "That's everything for today" when the last task is ticked. The aim is that opening the app lowers fear rather than adding to it.
  3. A moon, not an embryo. An early idea was a cute embryo character. It was rejected on ethical grounds: a large share of cycles fail, and a character the patient has bonded with would become a symbol of loss. Oy stands for the cycle, not the outcome — if a cycle fails, the moon simply begins again. Patients are never asked to attach to an embryo.

2.3.1 A word for today — 28 daily lines

Under the task list the home screen shows one short line, chosen by cycle day (day 1 → line 1 … day 28 → line 28), so every patient on the same day reads the same words and the line changes every morning. The lines follow the shape of the cycle: starting (1–5), the long middle of stimulation (6–13), retrieval and transfer (14–19), and the two-week wait (20–28). Three rules governed the writing: never promise an outcome ("it will work" is forbidden); name the feeling, do not fix it; and keep it under fifteen words so it reads in one glance and translates cleanly into Uzbek and Russian.

2.4 Data: collection, storage, protection

QuestionDesign
What is collectedCycle schedule (entered by the clinic), task completion, daily mood score and optional symptom chips, chat messages. Nothing else — no location, no contacts, no device data.
Who can see itThe patient and the clinic team, always. The partner only if the patient enables it, and then only the schedule — never mood or messages. Anonymised aggregate data only with separate consent.
Where it is storedOn a server operated by the clinic, inside Uzbekistan; no third-party analytics or cloud AI service receives patient text.
How it is protectedEncrypted in transit (TLS) and at rest; phone-number login with one-time SMS code; session expiry; role-based access (coordinator sees status, doctor sees medical queue).
How long it is keptDeleted 12 months after the cycle ends, or immediately on request.

2.5 AI, APIs and integration

CycleMate uses two narrow AI components, both on the clinic side and both deliberately limited:

  • Question router. A text classifier labels each incoming message as general (appointments, what to bring, what a step involves) or medical (doses, results, symptoms needing judgement). General questions are answered from the clinic's own FAQ; medical questions and anything the classifier is unsure about are forwarded to a human. The prototype implements this with keyword rules; a production version would use a small language model fine-tuned on anonymised clinic FAQs, with the same hard rule: when unsure, forward.
  • Call-list ranking. A simple score combining mood trend over the last three check-ins, missed home tasks and unanswered questions orders the coordinator's list. It changes who is called first; it does not label, diagnose or alter treatment.
The AI never diagnoses, never changes a dose, never interprets a result. Every clinical decision stays with the medical team. The companion exists to reduce fear, not to replace a clinician.

Integration. The prototype is stand-alone. The planned next step is a read-only connection to the clinic's electronic record so that scheduled visits and medication plans populate the timeline automatically, and an SMS gateway for offline reminders. No connection to national health systems is planned at this stage.

2.6 Feedback and what changed

The prototype was walked through with a clinic coordinator at a fertility clinic in Tashkent, screen by screen, in the coordinator's working environment.

Coordinator: "Patients who are struggling rarely message us — they just stop coming."
Change: the check-in logic was inverted. Instead of offering the patient a button to ask for help, two consecutive low check-ins now flag the coordinator, who calls the patient first. This became the core mechanism of the app.

Two further points from the same session were already covered by the design and were confirmed rather than changed: that roughly half of the questions coordinators receive are about doses and should go straight to the doctor (→ question router), and that cost is the most difficult conversation and usually comes too late (→ running total and the "worried about cost" chip).

Self-review (v1 → v3). The first prototype used a neutral timer-style interface. It worked, but it read as clinical and cold for an audience whose problem is anxiety. Version 3 added Oy, the warm palette and the "one task at a time" rule.

3Evaluation

3.1 Usability check

Formal usability testing with patients was out of scope for a two-day prototype and would require ethics approval. Instead an informal usability walkthrough was run with the clinic coordinator (n = 1) plus the author, covering every screen and the three main flows: completing today's plan, submitting a low check-in and seeing the flag, and asking a medical question and seeing it forwarded.

FlowResult
Login → consent → homeCompleted without instruction. Consent screen understood as "what the clinic sees"; partner toggle was the first thing the coordinator asked about ("patients will want this").
Today's plan → tick tasksImmediate. Oy's "that's everything for today" was noticed and liked.
Low check-in → flagThe flag message ("your coordinator will call you today, you don't need to do anything") was judged the single most useful element.
Medical question → forwardedRouting was understood at once; the coordinator confirmed this matches how questions should move inside the clinic.
Coordinator viewRanked list matched how the coordinator already prioritises informally; the "reason" line next to each name was requested to stay.

Conclusion of the session: the coordinator's assessment was that the workflow is realistic as it stands and could be run in actual clinical practice with the clinic's current team, without changing how the clinic works — the app formalises what the coordinator already tries to do by hand. The proposed next step is a small pilot (Part 3.5).

Limitation: n = 1 and no patients were involved. The results indicate that the design is coherent for clinic staff; patient-side usability, especially for low-literacy and older users, remains untested.

3.3 Privacy, security and ethical rules

  • Minimum data. Only what the features need; no tracking, no advertising identifiers.
  • Consent is specific and reversible. Separate toggles for clinic, partner and anonymised use, changeable at any time.
  • Clinic-hosted, in-country. No patient text leaves the clinic's infrastructure; no third-party cloud AI.
  • AI has a hard boundary. General information only; medical questions and uncertain cases go to a human; ranking changes call order, never care.
  • No labels. A flag is an instruction to the coordinator, not a diagnosis of the patient, and is never shown to anyone outside the care team.
  • Designed for failure. The companion represents the cycle, not the outcome, so a failed cycle does not turn the app into a reminder of loss.
  • Not a medical device. CycleMate gives no clinical advice and would be positioned as a patient-support tool, outside medical-device regulation; this would be confirmed with the relevant Uzbek authority before any pilot.

3.4 Health, access and equity

Health. The target outcome is cycle completion rate. If the burden-related share of drop-out (roughly one fifth of all discontinuations, and up to 28% in some cohorts) can be reduced even partly, more patients reach embryo transfer and a pregnancy test — the only point at which IVF can succeed. Secondary outcomes: check-in trend over the cycle, time from first low check-in to clinic contact, and number of proactive calls made.

Access. Web-based delivery works on any phone; SMS fallback covers patients without stable data. For patients outside Tashkent, the timeline makes explicit that only four visits are essential and that daily injections can be arranged at a partner clinic — turning "I can't come every day" from a reason to quit into a plan.

Equity. Three languages (EN / UZ / RU), icons and one task per screen for low-literacy users, and an optional partner mode so that a spouse who influences whether treatment continues can understand the process instead of questioning it.

3.5 How it could grow

  1. Pilot: 20 patients, one full cycle, at one clinic; compare completion and check-in data with the previous 20 cycles.
  2. EMR integration so schedules and medication plans populate automatically.
  3. Partner-clinic network for regional patients (injections and basic monitoring close to home).
  4. Full UZ / RU localisation, voice prompts for low-literacy users.
  5. Male-factor module — a timeline and check-in for male patients, who are currently left out of most fertility apps.
  6. Open the companion to other long, anxious treatment journeys (oncology, antenatal care) where the same "cycle, not outcome" logic applies.
